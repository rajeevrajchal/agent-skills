## Feature matrix

Build it from the research (date it, and cite the competitor pages):

| Feature / attribute | Product A | Product B | Product C | Open-source D | Proposed |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Core job 1 | ✅ | ✅ | ⚠️ partial | ✅ | ✅ |
| Core job 2 | ✅ | ❌ | ✅ | ❌ | ✅ |
| Setup time | ~1 day | ~1 hour | ~1 week | self-host | < 10 min |
| Entry price / month | €49 | €15 | €199 | free | €19 |
| Target segment | mid-market | freelancers | enterprise | devs | <niche> |
| <Differentiator candidate> | ❌ | ❌ | ❌ | ❌ | ✅ |
| User-requested feature X (from reviews) | ❌ | ⚠️ | ❌ | ❌ | ✅ |

Include **non-feature attributes** in the matrix (price, setup time, language and region support, compliance, integrations, support quality). Small products usually win on these, not on feature count.

Legend: ✅ supported · ⚠️ partial or poor · ❌ missing · ? unknown (verify before claiming).

## Feature classification

Sort every candidate feature:

| Class | Definition | Test |
|---|---|---|
| **Must-have (table stakes)** | Users won't consider a product without it | All serious competitors have it, and reviews punish its absence |
| **Differentiator** | A reason to choose you over alternatives | It addresses a repeated complaint or an ignored segment, and it's hard for incumbents to copy quickly or they don't want to |
| **Nice-to-have** | Pleasant but doesn't drive choice | Users mention it rarely; nobody switches for it |
| **Overbuilt** | Incumbents' complexity that your segment doesn't use | Reviews say "too complex" or "we only use 10%" |
| **Requested but missing** | Users repeatedly ask for it and competitors don't have it | Count the mentions; check the changelogs to confirm it's still missing |
| **Do not build (initially)** | Costs more than it returns at the MVP stage | Enterprise features (SSO, audit logs, granular roles) for an SMB product; native mobile apps when the web works; multi-language before one market works |

## Real advantage vs nice-to-have

A feature is a **real advantage** only if all of these hold:
1. It maps to a **repeated, evidenced pain** (reviews, conversations), not to founder taste.
2. It changes the **buying decision** for a definable segment ("we'd switch for that").
3. Competitors **can't or won't** copy it soon: it conflicts with their segment, pricing, architecture, or focus, or it depends on your niche knowledge, data, or distribution.
4. You can **communicate it in one sentence** that the target user cares about.

If it fails (3), it's a head start, not an advantage. Fine for a prototype, but you'll need distribution or depth to survive.

Common genuine advantages for small products: niche-specific workflow and terminology; local language, regulation, or payment support; radical simplicity and setup speed for a segment that incumbents overserve; a better price point for a segment incumbents ignore; integration with the niche's existing tools; and founder credibility in the community.

## Positioning options for small products

- **Niche down**: "<category> for <specific segment>" (with real workflow differences).
- **Simplify**: "the 20% of <incumbent> that <segment> actually uses, at 20% of the price".
- **Unbundle**: one heavily used feature of a suite, done better.
- **Localize**: language, currency, tax, and regulation for an underserved region.
- **Integrate**: the missing bridge between two tools the segment already uses.
- **Productize a service**: software plus done-for-you onboarding for non-technical buyers.

## Output

For each researched idea, produce:
1. The feature matrix (with dates and sources).
2. Lists of Must-have / Differentiators / Nice-to-have / Overbuilt / Requested-missing / Do-not-build.
3. A one-paragraph **gap statement**: "Incumbents serve <X> well but <segment> is underserved because <evidence>. A product focused on <differentiator> could win them if <assumption to validate>."
4. A **copy-risk** note: how quickly and how likely an incumbent is to close the gap.
