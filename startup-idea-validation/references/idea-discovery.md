# Idea Discovery

Don't generate random ideas. Find **problems people already spend time or money on**, then ask whether a small product could solve them better for a specific group.

## Problem statement format

Write every candidate as a problem statement first, never as a product:

```
[Specific user] struggles to [job/outcome] because [cause/gap],
which costs them [time/money/risk] about [frequency].
Today they [current workaround], which fails because [reason].
```

Example: "Independent physiotherapists in Denmark struggle to send exercise programs to patients because generic apps are English-only and don't match their billing system, which costs ~2 h/week of manual PDFs. Today they use Word templates and email, which patients lose."

If you can't fill in the brackets, the idea isn't ready to evaluate.

## Where good problems come from

Ordered roughly by signal quality:

1. **Money already being spent on manual work**: Upwork/Fiverr gigs repeated by many buyers, virtual assistants doing the same task, agencies selling a repeatable service. Strong signal: willingness to pay is proven.
2. **Complaints about existing paid tools**: 1–3 star reviews on G2, Capterra, Trustpilot, the App Store, Google Play, Chrome Web Store, and the Shopify App Store. Look for repeated themes such as "too expensive for small teams", "missing X", "too complex", "support is slow".
3. **Spreadsheets and duct tape**: communities sharing templates, Notion setups, or Zapier chains for a workflow is a sign of an unmet product need.
4. **Founder's own domain**: problems from jobs and industries the founder knows (domain knowledge is an advantage in itself), and the founder's own recurring pains, as long as others share them and pay to solve them.
5. **Community questions**: Reddit, Hacker News, Indie Hackers, Discord and Slack groups, industry forums, Stack Overflow. Recurring "how do you handle X?" threads.
6. **Shifts that create new needs**: new regulations (EU AI Act, accessibility requirements like the European Accessibility Act, e-invoicing mandates), platform changes (API deprecations, price hikes, a product sunset), and new technology making something cheap that used to be expensive.
7. **Unbundling**: one feature of a big, expensive suite that a niche uses heavily, offered simply and cheaply for that niche.
8. **"X for Y"**: an existing proven product adapted for an underserved vertical, language, region, or price point. This is valid only if the vertical has real differences, not just a different landing page.

## Search patterns for finding pain

Use with web search (and site filters where supported):
- `"is there a tool that" <domain>` · `"I wish there was" <task>` · `"how do you handle" <task> reddit`
- `<competitor> alternative` · `<competitor> "too expensive"` · `<competitor> review "doesn't"`
- `site:reddit.com <profession> "spreadsheet" <task>`
- `<task> template` (high template demand means a manual process)
- `site:upwork.com <task>` or freelancer listings for the task
- `<regulation> compliance small business` for new regulatory burdens
- `<platform> API deprecated` or `<product> shutting down` for orphaned users

Capture the evidence: link, date, and a quote. Several independent sources showing the same pain beats one viral post.

## Generation procedure

1. Start from the founder profile: domains known, communities with access, the preferred buyer type.
2. For each domain, list 5–10 recurring jobs and workflows. For each, ask: where is it slow, error-prone, expensive, or anxiety-inducing?
3. Search the patterns above for evidence of each pain (a light pass of 2–3 queries per problem at this stage).
4. Write problem statements. Discard any you can't complete.
5. Deduplicate and merge, then pass the list to screening (`problem-analysis.md`).

Aim for breadth at this stage: 10–50 problem statements, with no deep research yet.

## Idea shapes that suit solo founders

- B2B tools for a **specific vertical or role** with a clear budget owner (€20–200/month price points).
- **Workflow tools** replacing spreadsheets or manual steps in a niche.
- **Integrations and glue** between popular platforms where users ask for a missing connection (with platform risk in mind).
- **Productized services**: a service delivered with software leverage, which can become pure software later.
- **Developer tools** with a clear paid tier (hosted, team features, compliance), but the market is crowded and buyers are price-sensitive.
- **Data products**: aggregated, cleaned, niche data that's otherwise painful to gather (watch the legal terms of the sources).
- **Plugins and apps** in marketplaces with built-in distribution (Shopify, WordPress, Figma, Chrome, Slack, Atlassian), with platform dependency as the main risk.

## Idea shapes to treat with suspicion

- Social networks, marketplaces, and anything that needs network effects to deliver its first value.
- Consumer apps with no clear acquisition channel and low price points.
- "Platform for everyone" ideas without a beachhead segment.
- Ideas whose main feature the platform owner is likely to ship soon.
- Heavily regulated domains without domain expertise or a compliance budget.
- Hardware, unless the founder has the specific expertise and capital.
- Ideas the founder isn't interested in. Motivation over months matters.
